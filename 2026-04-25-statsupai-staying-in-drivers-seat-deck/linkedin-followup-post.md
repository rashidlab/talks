## The post

> If your AI use feels effortless, that might be the problem. Or it might be expertise plus good tool fit. The work is telling those two apart.
>
> I just gave a StatsUp.AI / ASA webinar on calibrating AI use in statistical research, training, and teaching. Part of the argument is old: tools change what we practice and what we let atrophy. Calculators changed mental arithmetic. GPS changed spatial memory. Search changed factual recall. Bainbridge's "Ironies of Automation" (1983) made the deeper point: automation rarely removes the human from a system. It usually leaves the human responsible for the harder residual judgment.
>
> But the analogy only goes so far. A calculator doesn't write your Methods section. AI is broader than the tools that came before because it can also produce the language and structure of judgment - and that makes it harder to see when the handoff happened.
>
> The risk isn't that AI gives a wrong answer. We already know how to worry about wrong answers. The quieter risk is that AI gives a usable answer while removing the practice that builds the judgment needed to evaluate it.
>
> Where does the line sit between hands-on and hands-off? My working test for statistical work: can you reconstruct, defend, and revise the work without asking the model again? Can you explain the estimand, the assumptions, the diagnostic checks, the failure modes, and what you'd do if the result were wrong? If not, the model has moved from assisting your judgment to standing in for it.
>
> Some of that line is personal. It moves with the task, the stakes, and where you are in your career. A first-year PhD student needs a different line than a senior researcher. A regulated trial needs a different line than scratch work. But not all of it is personal. Some has to be set by labs, courses, journals, sponsors, and regulators, because individual comfort isn't the same as accountability.
>
> The cleanest empirical example I know is Bastani et al. (PNAS, 2025). A field experiment with nearly a thousand high-school math students, randomized to three groups: GPT-4 with no guardrails, GPT-4 with tutor-style prompts, or no AI. Both AI groups performed better while the tool was available. On the unassisted final exam, the no-guardrails group dropped 17% below the no-AI control. The tutor-prompt group was indistinguishable from control. Same model, different scaffolding, very different learning. It's high-school math, not graduate statistics, but the direction is hard to wave away.
>
> The practices I argued for are deliberately unglamorous: write your own plan before prompting; make the model expose assumptions rather than skip to answers; keep a decision log for analytic choices; explain the result without looking at the transcript; and red-team the final product as if it came from a collaborator you don't fully trust. The point isn't to make AI use slower for its own sake. It's to keep enough friction in the workflow that judgment still has somewhere to live.
>
> Slides and a companion repo (templates, checklists, prompt patterns, full bibliography) are public. Links in the first comment.
>
> If you know faculty, advisors, or PhD students wrestling with these questions, pass it along. The materials are free, and the conversation gets better when more people are in it.
